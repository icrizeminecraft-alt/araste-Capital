Financing a property project in stages

How is a property project financed, from the land to practical completion? Each phase changes the risk of the transaction, and therefore the funding that can be raised. This guide describes what each stage means for the financing, the role of equity, the possible exits and the points a lender examines.

5 min read

Key points

  • 01Each phase of a project changes the risk, and therefore the form of financing available.
  • 02The budget must be complete, including financing costs and a contingency.
  • 03Equity absorbs the first losses and evidences the sponsor's commitment.
  • 04The chosen exit determines the repayment profile, the security and the type of lender.
  • 05A permission beyond challenge and pre-sales reduce the uncertainties a lender will raise.

01

The phases of a project

A property project unfolds in distinct phases, and each one changes the risk the transaction carries. As long as the land is not under control and the consents are still open to challenge, the lender is financing an intention. Once the works have started, it is financing a building site, with its technical uncertainties. At practical completion, it is financing an asset that exists but does not yet produce anything. Sales or letting then close the cycle. The financing follows this progression: its form, its security and its cost evolve as the uncertainty recedes.

  • Control of the land: an option, an acquisition or the contribution of a building already held.
  • Consents: application, grant, then the expiry of third-party challenge and administrative review periods.
  • Works: tendering to contractors, start on site, progress and handover.
  • Practical completion: the asset is finished, but the construction debt still has to be repaid.
  • Sales or letting: the exit takes place and the financing is unwound.

02

The overall budget and contingencies

The budget presented to a lender covers the whole transaction, from the land to the exit, and not only the cost of the works. It brings together the price of the land or the building, the works, professional fees, taxes, financing costs and sales costs. It must also include a contingency, because a building site almost always meets the unexpected. A budget presented without one worries more than it reassures. It suggests that the project sponsor will discover the overruns along the way, at the point where they are hardest to finance.

Financing costs deserve particular attention. They depend on the timetable: every month of delay extends the period during which the debt runs without the asset producing any income. A realistic timetable, phase by phase, is therefore a component of the budget in its own right. It makes it possible to check that the planned margin withstands a later completion or slower sales than hoped.

03

The role of equity

Equity is the share of the budget that the sponsor commits itself, ahead of or alongside the debt: cash, land already paid for or a building contributed to the transaction. It absorbs the first losses if the project goes wrong, which protects the lender, and evidences the sponsor's commitment. A lender most often expects it to be committed first, before the debt is drawn down, and its origin and availability to be documented. Its level is not set in advance; it depends on the phase, the status of consents and the chosen exit.

04

The possible exits

The exit is the way the debt will be repaid. It shapes the structure of the financing from the outset: repayment profile, security, type of lender. A block sale passes the whole building to a single buyer, often an investor; it simplifies the unwinding but depends on a narrower market. Individual unit sales dispose of the scheme unit by unit; they spread the receipts and the repayment. Letting followed by a refinancing replaces the construction debt with long-term debt backed by the rents. Each exit has its fallback routes, which are best identified before approaching a lender.

05

What a lender looks at

A lender first examines the team: the sponsor's track record on comparable projects, the strength of the lead consultant, the contractors and the sales agent. It then looks at the status of consents, because a permission beyond challenge removes one of the main uncertainties. It takes an interest in pre-sales: reservations or a lease signed before completion reduce the exit risk. Finally, it assesses the timetable and how the funds will be drawn. As a rule, construction is financed in tranches, against evidence of progress, which requires close monitoring of the site and of the contractors' applications for payment.

  • The team's experience on transactions of a comparable nature and size.
  • The status of consents and whether any challenge is under way.
  • The level of pre-sales or pre-letting at the date of the request.
  • The consistency of the timetable with the planned drawdowns.
  • The quality of the security: mortgage, share pledge, guarantees, assignment of receivables.

06

Common mistakes

Some mistakes recur. Seeking financing too early, before the land is under control or the planning application has been lodged, leads to indications of interest without commitment. Presenting a budget without a contingency, or a timetable without any margin, weakens the credibility of the file. Relying on a single exit, with no fallback, exposes the transaction to a market downturn. Finally, overlooking the period after practical completion, during which the debt is still running, is a frequent source of strain. An adviser can help you spot these points before a lender raises them.

  • Does your timetable state, phase by phase, when each source of funding must be available?
  • Does your budget cost a later completion or slower sales than planned?
  • Can you evidence the origin and availability of the equity to be committed first?
  • Have you costed the fallback route if the chosen exit does not materialise?

These guides are educational. They constitute neither personalised advice, nor an offer of financing, nor a legal or tax opinion. Every transaction calls for its own analysis.
